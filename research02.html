Dynamic Equity Slope – R&R
  with M. Breugem, S. Colonnello & F. Zucchi

Abstract: This paper empirically documents that expected growth volatility is a key driver of the equity term structure dynamics. A general equilibrium model jointly explains four important patterns: (i) a potentially negative unconditional equity term premium, (ii) countercyclical equity term premia, (iii) procyclical equity yields, and (iv) premia to value and growth claims respectively increasing and flat with the horizon. The economic mechanism hinges on the interaction between heteroscedastic long-run growth--- which leads to countercyclical risk premia---and homoscedastic short-term shocks under limited market participation---which produce sizable risk premia to short-term cash flows. The equity slope dynamics hold irrespective of the sign of its unconditional average.

News Duration Risk and Asset Prices

Abstract: This paper studies asset pricing when investors observe the size and frequency of aggregate shocks but disagree about their duration. Motivated by survey evidence that professional forecasters differ in the speed at which they expect output shocks to dissipate, I develop a continuous-time endowment economy in which agents agree on the probabilities and magnitudes of transitory news shocks but hold different priors over their decay rate. A shock therefore starts a learning episode: its impact is observed immediately, while its persistence is revealed only through the subsequent recovery path. The equilibrium separates size and probability risks from news-duration risk. Risk sharing is likelihood-weighted, and the stochastic discount factor depends on both aggregate marginal utility and the consumption-weighted distribution of beliefs. Duration learning reallocates consumption and wealth, changes the beliefs of the marginal investor, and moves discount rates, risk premia, and hedging demands. In a three-decay-rate implementation, persistent-duration evidence raises the relevance of future cash-flow consequences and strengthens predictability, whereas evidence of rapid dissipation compresses compensation by shortening the expected life of the shock. These effects appear in valuation ratios, derivative positions, and bond and equity yields. The analysis shows that news-duration risk is a distinct asset-pricing force: even when investors agree on what happened, disagreement about how long it will matter is priced.

Irreversible Capital Reallocation and the Term Structure of Transition Risk

Abstract: The low-carbon transition requires firms to retire productive carbon-intensive assets and finance cleaner replacements. We ask how the speed of this process and the allocation of its costs determine when investors bear the resulting losses and gains. We develop a production economy with installed brown and green capital and irreversible replacement. Replacement destroys one productive position while creating another, so the optimal trigger compares the continuation values of both stocks. A separate drift condition determines whether brown capital survives in the long run. Limited implementation capacity creates a region in which replacement is desirable but constrained. We value the cash flows generated by this allocation in a common consumption-service numeraire and decompose them by maturity. This decomposition defines the term structure of transition risk. Physical adjustment determines aggregate cash-flow timing, but financing determines which security bears short-horizon losses. Duration and transition exposure therefore depend on both technology and contract. A green claim can change the sign of its exposure across maturities when it finances installation, while the same real allocation produces a different exposure when financing is assigned elsewhere. U.S. generator histories motivate the model's persistent stocks, productive retirements, distinct retirement and entry flows, and gradual adjustment. The analysis shows why technology labels alone are insufficient for measuring transition risk.

Corporate Policies and the Term Structure of Risk
  with M. Breugem & F. Zucchi

Abstract: Asset pricing research indicates that the long and short term do not contribute equally to the market risk premium, and that their relative contribution is timevarying. Having notable implications for firm discount rates, this paper studies the corporate finance implications of this line of work. In a dynamic model with financing frictions, we show that firms should extend (shorten) their horizon if short-term shocks have a greater (smaller) market price than long-term ones, i.e., if the term structure of risk prices is downward-sloping (upward-sloping). Ignoring a downward-sloping term structure leads to underinvestment, excessive payouts, inadequate cash reserves and equity issuances, and excessive liquidations.

Labor Rigidity and the Dynamics of the Value Premium
 

Abstract: This paper documents that (i) the labor-share is a strong predictor of both the value and duration premia, (ii) these premia are highly correlated, and (iii) the labor-share does not forecast the component of the value premium orthogonal to the duration premium. A simple equilibrium model with labor rigidity and heterogeneity in cash-flow durations rationalizes these stylized facts. The economic channel is a term-structure effect: labor rigidity boosts short-run dividend risk because wages are more responsive to permanent than transitory shocks. This leads to downward-sloping equity risk and to a cross-sectional duration premium. In turn, value firms earn a compensation over growth firms which is predicted by labor-share variation.

Survey Expectations and the Risk-Return Trade-Off
  with M. Breugem

Abstract: Intuition and leading equilibrium models are at odds with the empirical evidence that expected returns are weakly related to volatility at the market level. This paper proposes a closed-form general equilibrium model, which connects the investors' expectations of fundamentals with those of market returns, as documented by survey data. Forecasts suggest that investors feature pro-cyclical optimism and, then, overestimate the persistence of aggregate risk. In bad times, the forward-looking component of stock volatility offset the transient risk and leads to a weak risk-return relation, in line with survey data about market returns. The model mechanism is robust to many features of financial markets.

Inflation Risk, Labor Rigidity, and Bond Returns
 

Abstract: This paper exploits information from the variance-ratios of macroeconomic variables to infer about the short and long-run components of dividend risk and inflation risk. While labor rigidity shifts dividend risk towards the short horizon, it also reveals --by means of labor-share variation-- the component of inflation risk which is correlated with fundamentals. A simple general equilibrium model with labor rigidity can explain how inflation interacts with the real growth and the labor-share, as well as many patterns of the term-structures of real and nominal bond yields. The model is robust to many properties of equity returns.

Demand Shocks, Timing Preferences, and the Equilibrium Term Structures
 

Abstract: Leading asset pricing models fail to describe the downward-sloping term-structure of equity. This paper proposes a closed-form framework where both supply- and demand-shocks contribute to equilibrium prices under either full or incomplete information. The joint role of timing preferences and demand-shocks on the equilibrium term-structures of bond and equity is characterized. The model reconciles standard asset pricing facts, such as the low and smooth risk-free rate, the high equity premium and volatility, with positive and negative slopes respectively of bond and equity. Under incomplete information, the minimum requirement on information quality, which preserves the slopes of the term-structures, is derived. Information quality barely affects the unconditional moments of equity returns but is crucial to their term-structure properties, which appear as an important diagnostics of asset pricing frameworks.

Rational Learning and Term Structures
  with M. Hasler and M. Khapko (permanent working paper)

Abstract: We study the impact of information processing and rational learning on the term structures of equity risk, risk premia, and bond yields. In opposition to the full information economy, learning yields an upward-sloping term structure of bond yields and downward-sloping term structures of equity risk and risk premia. Moreover, learning yields lower interest rates and a larger risk premium than in an otherwise identical economy with full information. Therefore, information processing and learning helps explain jointly the observed level and timing of both equity return moments and interest rates. Economic growth forecast data lend support to our model of learning.
